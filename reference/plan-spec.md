# Plan specification

What the generated plan contains, in build order. Each section constrains the next, so don't reorder.

---

## 1. The positioning line

One sentence: what they'd be the obvious person to ask about.

Draw it from questions 1, 4 and 5 together. The territory sits where their **receipts**, their **interest** and their **disagreements** overlap. Any two without the third fails: receipts plus interest with no disagreement is a résumé; interest plus disagreement with no receipts is noise.

Narrow ruthlessly. If the line could describe more than about fifty people, it's still a field, not a territory.

If you can't write this from their answers, ask one more question rather than inventing it. Everything downstream inherits this sentence.

## 2. Pillars

Three to five. Everything they publish for 90 days maps to one.

Derive from questions 4 and 5, anchored by the receipts in question 1. Never more than five — past that nobody remembers what the person is about, including the person.

Demote anything that's a practice rather than a thesis. "I write code" is not a pillar; it's evidence for one.

State explicitly: if something doesn't map to a pillar, it doesn't ship. Range is what makes people forget you.

## 3. The take inventory

12–20 numbered positions built from question 5. Each one gets:

- **The claim** — one sentence, hedges stripped
- **The evidence** — a number, a named product behaviour, a study, or one of their own receipts
- **The rebuttal they must be able to answer** — the strongest version of the counter-case, stated as a smart opponent would put it, then the answer

Number them. Numbers become stable identifiers the rest of the plan refers to, and the person will use them in conversation.

Calibrate heat to question 8. A take they won't publish is worth nothing; a take too mild to argue with is worth almost as little.

Research for real evidence. **Never invent a statistic.** If a take needs a number and none exists, rebuild the take around something they can prove from their own work.

Include the defensibility test they run before posting:

1. Can I name the evidence in one sentence?
2. Can I state the opposing case fairly, and answer it?
3. Would I say this in a final-round interview at a company I want to work for?
4. Am I attacking an idea, or a person? If a person, it doesn't ship.

## 4. The daily schedule

Hour by hour, built from question 7 at **80% of stated hours**, with at least one day off.

Structure it backwards from the publishing slot, which comes from question 9. Protect the hour after publishing for replies — on most platforms the first sixty minutes decide reach, which makes that hour worth more than the writing.

Give each block a line on **why it exists**. A schedule without reasons gets abandoned the first time something else comes up; a schedule whose logic they understand survives a bad week.

Include the rest argument explicitly, as arithmetic rather than kindness: 90 days is the unit, and someone who burns out in week five produces less than someone who finishes.

## 5. Platform playbooks

At most three, chosen from their links and audience. Mechanics from their follower counts, timing from their audience's geography.

Per platform: cadence, format rotation, post construction, what gets suppressed, and how much time daily.

**Research current platform mechanics rather than relying on memory.** Algorithms change and stale advice is worse than none. Say which figures are estimates and tell them to override with their own analytics after two weeks.

## 6. The project slate

Things to build that sharpen their craft, produce publishable evidence, and reinforce the territory.

**Extend what already exists before inventing something new.** A half-built thing finished ships sooner and reads better.

List more than they can do, then say plainly how many to pick — usually three or four in 90 days. Naming the over-commitment failure mode is part of the value: being 60% into six projects is the standard way ambitious quarters produce nothing.

For each: what it is, roughly how long, and **what it buys them** — the credential, not the activity.

## 7. Phases and gates

Four phases across 90 days. Each gets a **gate**: a checkable condition, not an intention. "Published 6 posts and 3 case studies are live" is a gate. "Feeling more confident" is not.

Typical shape, adjusted per person:

- **Days 1–14** — build the arsenal, set the positioning, publish from existing work
- **Days 15–45** — volume. This is where people quit
- **Days 46–75** — authority. Become something people cite rather than someone with opinions
- **Days 76–90** — conversion. Turn attention into conversations

## 8. Scoreboard and kill rules

One metric that actually matters — usually **inbound conversations they didn't initiate**. Name the vanity metrics explicitly as things to log but not optimise.

Kill rules, applied on a fixed day, without sentiment:

- A post format under ~2% engagement three times running gets dropped for four weeks
- A project with no publishable artifact in two weeks gets cut or rescoped
- A pillar with no posts in a fortnight gets scheduled or removed
- A target account replied to 15+ times with no acknowledgement comes off the list

Without these, the plan is a wish list.

## 9. The honest expectation

Close with it. Weeks one to four feel like shouting into a void, because they are. Compounding starts somewhere around week four to six. **The people who quit, quit in week three** — and knowing that in advance is most of what gets someone through it.
