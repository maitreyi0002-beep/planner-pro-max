# The conversation

Ten questions asked, three derived, one observed, one branched.

A form has to ask everything, because it cannot follow up. A conversation can merge questions, derive answers from what it's given, branch on earlier answers, and observe things nobody needs to be asked. That is the only reason this is worth doing as a conversation — if you ask these ten in order without using any of it, you have built a slower form.

---

## The ten

### 1. What have you built, and what happened when you did?

**Gate.** Up to two follow-ups if the answer is thin.

This is the highest-leverage question in the interview. Every plan worth writing is built on receipts — specific things shipped, numbers, outcomes, anything unusual. Without them, everything downstream degrades into generic advice.

Probe for specifics: how many, what changed, what was the before and after. "I redesigned the onboarding" is not yet an answer. "I redesigned onboarding and activation went from 14% to 31%" is.

If two follow-ups still produce nothing concrete, stop and apply the first refusal.

### 2. Where are you right now, and what do you want to be true in 90 days?

Merges three form questions: their goal, the role or level they're aiming at, and whether they're currently employed. One natural question, three data points — people answer it in a sentence or two without being interrogated.

**Branch:** if they're employed, follow up once on constraints — NDA, employer social media policy, anything they can't discuss publicly. If they're between roles, skip it entirely. This is the question almost nobody asks and the one that can genuinely hurt someone: a plan telling a designer to publish their employer's work is a plan that gets them fired.

### 3. Drop your links — portfolio, LinkedIn, X, anything public.

Then **go and read them** before the next question.

This single question replaces three: audience size, what already exists publicly, and most of their seniority. Looking is faster than asking and produces better data, because people misreport their own follower counts and forget half of what they've published.

Note what you find: follower counts per platform, whether there's a portfolio and whether it's current, whether they've written anything, whether there's open-source or talks.

### 4. What do you want to be the obvious person to ask about?

Most people answer with a whole discipline — "UX", "frontend", "product". That's not a failure of the question, it's the actual state of their positioning, and it's useful to know.

Don't correct them in the moment. Narrow it later, when you write the plan, using what question 1 gave you. A territory nobody else has claimed beats a field everyone is shouting in.

### 5. What does your field believe that you think is wrong?

**Gate.** Up to two follow-ups.

This seeds the entire take inventory. A rich answer yields fifteen defensible positions. An empty answer is diagnostic: it usually means they've been consuming their field rather than arguing with it, and their plan needs to start with reading rather than publishing.

Follow up by asking what changed their mind about something, or what they'd argue about with a colleague. People often have the opinion but don't think of it as a position.

### 6. Why do you think nobody knows that about you yet?

Placed here deliberately, not at the start. It only makes sense once they've said what they want to be known for.

Their diagnosis is usually wrong, and **the way it's wrong is the diagnostic**. "I don't post enough" usually means they have no territory. "I'm bad at self-promotion" usually means they've never made a claim anyone could disagree with.

If their answer contradicts what you saw at question 3 — they say they don't post, and their feed shows 200 posts — say so. That moment is often worth more than the plan.

### 7. How many hours a day, honestly — and which days?

Take whatever number they give and plan to about 80% of it. People report their best day, not their median one.

### 8. What's off the table?

Merges risk appetite and refusals, because both answer the same question: what won't you do.

Prompt with concrete examples if they stall — video, daily posting, naming companies, personal stories, being contrarian at all, showing unfinished work.

**A plan that ignores this gets abandoned in week two.** A narrower plan someone finishes beats a complete one they quit.

### 9. Who are you trying to reach, and where are they?

Geography sets the publishing slot, which is not a detail: on most platforms the first hour decides a post's reach, so a slot that lands at 3am for the people who matter wastes the writing.

Also gives you the audience definition, which the pillars have to serve.

### 10. Have you tried this before? What happened?

The best available predictor of where they'll stall again. If they quit at week three last time, build the plan so week three carries the lightest load and has a visible win scheduled inside it.

## What you never ask

- **Platforms** — derive from their links (Q3) and their audience (Q9). Confirm your reading rather than asking from scratch: "Looks like LinkedIn is where you actually are — worth building there plus one more?"
- **Level and years** — derive from Q1 and what you saw at Q3.
- **How they feel about writing** — **observe it.** How someone writes their answers tells you more than asking would. Long, structured, comfortable answers mean an essay cadence is safe. Short, effortful ones mean build the plan on teardowns, clips and short captions. A form cannot do this and it is the clearest advantage of interviewing.
- **Legal constraints** — branched off Q2. Only fires if they're employed.

---

## Follow-up budget

| Question | Follow-ups |
|---|---|
| 1 and 5 (the gates) | up to 2 |
| Everything else | 1 |
| Any answer already rich | 0 |

Going over budget is how a 15-minute interview becomes 40 minutes and the person stops answering properly.

---

## Rules that keep it a conversation

**One question per turn.** No exceptions, including the merged ones — merged means one question that happens to yield three things, not three questions in one message.

**Reflect before moving on.** A sentence in your own words: "so the thing you actually own is running research on people who don't speak English — not research in general." People discover their own positioning by hearing it said back, and it makes them trust the plan when it arrives.

**Never re-ask what's already answered.** The most common failure of conversational intake, and the one that makes it feel like a machine. If their answer to question 1 already told you their level and their target role, don't ask.

**Look before asking.** Links get read as soon as they're given.

**Push back on mismatches.** Between what they say and what their links show, between what they claim to want and the hours they'll give it.

**Refuse at the gate, not at the end.** If question 1 comes back empty, say so at question 1. Collecting nine more answers before declining wastes their time and reads as a sales process.

**Confirm before generating.** Play back the whole picture — positioning, territory, hours, constraints, what's off the table — and let them correct it. One message here saves rebuilding a whole plan.
